Every route on Kilimanjaro leads to the same place: Uhuru Peak, 5,895 m above sea level on the rim of the Kibo crater. Yet the experience of getting there varies enormously. The route you choose shapes the scenery you walk through, the number of people you share the trail with and, most importantly, how well your body adapts to altitude.

This guide covers the five routes we offer and how to decide between them.

## Why the route matters

Kilimanjaro is a walk, not a technical climb, so the main challenge is altitude rather than difficulty. As you gain height, the air holds less oxygen, and your body needs time to adjust. Routes that climb gradually, or that follow the principle of climbing high during the day and sleeping lower, give your body that time.

> The single most important decision you make is not which route, but how many days. An extra day on the mountain is the best investment you can make in reaching the summit.

Shorter itineraries are cheaper, but they ask more of your body and summit success rates are lower. We generally recommend at least seven days on the mountain, and eight where possible.

## The routes in detail

### Machame

[Machame](/kilimanjaro/machame) is the most popular route on the mountain, and it is easy to see why. It climbs through dense rainforest to the Shira Plateau, then traverses the southern slopes beneath the glaciers. Highlights include the Lava Tower and the scramble up the Barranco Wall, which is steep but not technical.

The route has a good acclimatisation profile, particularly on a seven-day itinerary, but it is busy, especially in peak months. If you enjoy the company of other climbers and want a varied, classic route, Machame is a strong choice.

### Lemosho

[Lemosho](/kilimanjaro/lemosho) is our most frequently recommended route. It begins on the remote western side of the mountain, in forest where you may see colobus monkeys, and the first days are notably quiet. It crosses the full width of the Shira Plateau before joining the southern circuit towards the summit.

On eight days, Lemosho offers an excellent acclimatisation profile, superb scenery and a gradual build-up that suits most climbers. It is a little more expensive because of the longer itinerary and the drive to the trailhead, but we believe it is worth it.

### Marangu

[Marangu](/kilimanjaro/marangu) is the only route with hut accommodation, and it follows the same path up and down. It is sometimes called the easiest route, but that reputation is misleading: it is often climbed in five days, which gives limited time to acclimatise. On six days, with an extra night at Horombo Hut, it becomes a much better option.

Marangu suits those who would rather sleep in a hut than a tent, and the rainy season when huts offer more comfort.

### Rongai

[Rongai](/kilimanjaro/rongai) approaches from the north, close to the Kenyan border. The northern slopes are drier than the south, which makes Rongai a good choice in the rainier months, and the route is quieter than Machame or Lemosho. The scenery is gentler and less varied, but the sense of wilderness is real.

Rongai descends via the Marangu route, so you see two sides of the mountain.

### Northern Circuit

The [Northern Circuit](/kilimanjaro/northern-circuit) is the longest route on Kilimanjaro, and the most complete. It begins like Lemosho in the west, then circles around the quiet northern slopes before approaching the summit from the north-east. It has the best acclimatisation profile of any route and very few other climbers.

If you have the time, it is our favourite way to experience the mountain.

## How to decide

Most climbers can narrow their choice by answering three questions.

1. **How many days do you have?** If you can manage eight or nine days on the mountain, Lemosho or the Northern Circuit are excellent. With six or seven, Machame or Rongai work well.
2. **How much do you value solitude?** Lemosho, Rongai and the Northern Circuit are noticeably quieter than Machame.
3. **When are you travelling?** In the wetter months, the drier northern slopes of Rongai can make a real difference.

Fitness matters too, but less than you might expect. Regular hill walking in the months before your climb is the best preparation, and the slow pace on the mountain helps everyone.

## Safety and crew welfare

Whatever route you choose, the quality of your crew matters as much as the trail. Our partner crews are led by experienced, qualified guides trained in wilderness first aid. They check every climber's health twice a day, carry pulse oximeters and emergency oxygen, and will always descend with anyone showing signs of serious altitude illness.

We also care about the people who carry the loads. Our crews follow porter welfare standards aligned with the Kilimanjaro Porters Assistance Project, including fair wages, proper equipment, sensible load limits and good food and shelter.

## Before and after the climb

A night at a lodge near Moshi or Arusha before you start lets you rest after the flight and check your kit. Climbing [Mount Meru](/experiences/mount-meru-trek) beforehand is an excellent way to acclimatise. After the summit, many climbers add a short safari in the northern parks or a few days on [Zanzibar](/zanzibar).
